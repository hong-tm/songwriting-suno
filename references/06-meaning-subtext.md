# 06 · Meaning, Subtext & Symbolism

Reference for layering implied meaning under a lyric: theme vs subject, subtext, double meanings, symbolism, motif, unreliable narration, and music-vs-lyric irony. Pair with 05 for the sonic half and the Suno docs (10-13) for prompting.

## Quick rules / TL;DR

- Separate subject (literal topic), theme (the strategy directing the journey), message (what the listener should do/feel/notice), story (the narrative delivery). One song can hold all four plus an implication layer.
- Make every line do two jobs (text + subtext). Bad dialogue, and bad lines, "do only one thing at once" (Francine Prose).
- Recolor one flexible hook or image across verses instead of restating it; the same line should absorb the additional meanings the other verses supply (Pattison's "Time to Let Go").
- Pick titles with DNA that already imply more than one reading ("Heartbreak Hotel": a place you check into, check out of, or go after your heart breaks).
- A true double entendre keeps both readings live; if the hidden meaning kills the surface meaning, it's a single entendre.
- The most reliable irony machine is the gap between music and lyric: happy music + dark lyric lands the darkness *more* effectively because the melody carries the listen.
- Author intent and audience meaning are different layers and can legitimately diverge ("Every Breath You Take", "Born in the U.S.A."); write deliberately, then let the listener ratify.

## 1. Theme vs subject vs message vs story

| Concept | Definition | Test question | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject | The concrete topic the song is about | "What is this literally about?" | Elvis Costello's five subjects: "I'm leaving you. You're leaving me. I want you. You don't want me. I believe in something." |
| Theme | The vision/strategy directing the lyric's journey | "What is it really exploring?" | "Anarchy in the UK", "Strange Fruit", "I Want to Hold Your Hand" |
| Message | What the writer wants the listener to do/feel/notice | "What is being solicited?" | "notice the homeless, start a revolution, or dance the night away" |
| Story | Narrative delivery: plot, incident, or ambience | "Is there a who-did-what-when?" | "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald", "Stan", "The Gambler" |

Rules:

- Theme is a strategy, not a sentence, per Charlotte Yates (Soundfly): tactics sit on a spectrum from **obscurity (sonics over sense) → incident/moment → full plot**. Choose the point on the spectrum *before* writing lines.
- Prosody is the master rule (Pattison, via Sound on Sound): form and content must agree, or deliberately disagree. "If the broken-heartedness had been supported by the musical and lyrical structure, I would be feeling more."
- Point of view is the lever. Pattison's four POVs: third-person narrative, second-person, first-person, direct address, each changing what the song can mean and withhold. "Eleanor Rigby" (3rd), "Like a Rolling Stone" (2nd), "Hotel California" (1st), love songs (direct address).
- Chorus answers verse abstractly, not literally (Gary Ewer): verse = story, chorus = the emotion the story generates, bridge = "then-this-happened → so-I-felt-this."

## 2. Subtext and implication

Definition (DiAnn Mills): "characters who talk about one thing but really mean something else, and they both know it... an unspoken conversation below a verbal conversation and more valuable than the spoken word."

Why writers use it: discretion (the wrong listener must not understand); lack of courage to state it directly; a message meant only for a select few (dog-whistle/coded meaning); an ulterior motive. What it buys: information without exposition; tension; a second plot layer; character insight; mystery; foreshadowing; it lets the audience determine meaning, "shows the reader that the writer respects their intelligence."

Workhorse moves:

- Say the literal thing, mean the emotional thing. A breakup scene conducted entirely in talk of paperwork or weather.
- Let a detail betray the speaker's lie (the suspiciously specific denial: Justin Bieber's "Sorry", "missing more than just your body").
- Bury the real question at the end of a list of throwaway ones (50 Cent, "21 Questions": the cheating question is the only one that mattered).
- Confession disguised as compliment (Stone Poneys, "Different Drum": "I ain't sayin' you ain't pretty...").
- Self-interrogation whose answer shifts (Jimmy Buffett, "Margaritaville": "just whose fault is this, anyway?").

Ambiguity as a feature, per Michael Stipe: "I've always felt the best kinds of songs are the ones where anybody can listen to it, put themselves in it and say, 'Yeah, that's me.'" Song Exploder primary quote, Lykke Li on "I Follow Rivers": the title phrase never appears in the lyric, "I think it's more about the theme and the currents of a dark love" — the title names the theme, the text never states it.

## 3. Double meanings, wordplay, double entendre

The term is French ("double understanding"), first used in English by Dryden, 1673. The rule: **two or more meanings live at once**, and the innuendo need not be sexual. It overlaps pun, homophone, euphemism, innuendo.

The critical distinction, Willa Paskin (Vulture): Katy Perry's "Peacock" is "the most egregious example of the entirely unsubtle double entendre... She used a common word for penis and made it mean penis!" A single entendre collapses to one reading; keep both payoffs.

| Song | Artist | Mechanism | Meanings in play |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Hound Dog" (1952/56) | Big Mama Thornton → Elvis | extended metaphor + sexual entendre | Leiber/Stoller wrote it as a woman berating her selfish exploitative man; "hound dog" = gigolo. Elvis's version defanged it into a generic put-down, losing the gender-power subtext. |
| "Whole Lotta Love" (1969) | Led Zeppelin | euphemism + entendre | "You need cooling... you need love" / "every inch of my love": instructional surface, carnal subtext; also a plagiarism case (Willie Dixon's "You Need Love"). |
| "You Shook Me All Night Long" (1980) | AC/DC | double entendre | two readings sustained across the whole lyric |
| "If U Seek Amy" (2008) | Britney Spears | homophone | "F-U-C-K me"; surface phrase makes no sense otherwise |
| "LoveGame" (2009) | Lady Gaga | euphemism | "disco stick" |
| "Right Round" (2009) | Flo Rida | innuendo | "when you go down" + "head", over-signposted |
| "Press My Button (Ring My Bell)" (1930s) | Lil Johnson | blues entendre | doorbells/plugs on the surface, sex underneath |
| "Milkshake" (2003) | Kelis | vague entendre | "relatively vague," per Vulture |

How to write one: choose a surface register fully coherent on its own; embed a second register reachable only through a trigger word; keep both payoffs.

## 4. Symbolism, motif, metaphor, allusion

### Motif = the recoloring engine

Pick a flexible image and let it shift color as the song travels. Pattison's demonstration, "Time to Let Go": the same chorus line ("destined to soar") means the child's future from the parent's side in verse 1, from the child's side in verse 2, and becomes a quotation of the dying parent in the final chorus. Rule: "each of the lines should be able to absorb the additional meanings supplied by the other verses." His title-selection test is literally "DNA" — a good title already implies concepts. "Heartbreak Hotel" is his worked example: a place you check into or out of, where nobody actually lives, where your heart breaks, or where you go after.

### Metaphor (Pattison, "Making Metaphors")

- "In its most basic form, metaphor is a collision between ideas that don't belong together."
- "All metaphors must be literally false." If the two things are identical, it's a definition. "Conflict is essential."
- Three types: **expressed identity** ("fear is a shadow"; "the shadow of fear"; "fear's shadow"), **qualifying metaphor** ("hasty clouds"), **verbal metaphor** ("clouds sail"; "frost gobbles summer down").
- Method: metaphor reveals a third thing two ideas share. Ask (1) what characteristics does my idea have? (2) what else has those?
- Verbs are the power amplifiers: "Be careful what you attach your amplifier to!" (Frost's "shouldering its way and shedding the earth crumbs").
- Sense-bound specificity generates identification: "the more specific you get, the more sense-bound you get, the more effective it is" — "You'd be so nice by the fire"; "Turn down the lights, turn down the bed, turn down these voices inside my head."
- Object writing is the metaphor engine: 10 timed minutes across seven senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic, kinesthetic), sense-bound free association.

### Allusion / intertextuality

Songs that lean on prior texts so the image does the emotional work:

- Taylor Swift, "The Fate of Ophelia" — Shakespeare's Ophelia: flowers, drowning, madness ( Elle's meaning breakdown).
- Coldplay, "Viva la Vida" — historical king persona, "Jerusalem bells," "Roman cavalry choirs" (visual-semiotic reading, small-journal study).
- Rihanna, "Diamonds" — the diamond as shifting signifier (semiotic study).
- Caveat: the semiotic-journal analyses are peer-reviewed but small-journal; they show method more than settled authorial intent.

## 5. Narrative techniques

### Unreliable narrator

The mechanism is always the gap between what the narrator asserts and what the details reveal.

- "The Old Apartment" (Barenaked Ladies): sounds like a stalker break-in; the twist is he's broken into his own former home out of nostalgia.
- "Sorry" (Justin Bieber): suspiciously specific denial reveals the opposite.
- "Different Drum": compliment as confession.
- "Margaritaville": escalating self-exculpation undercut by the obvious.

### Dramatic irony (listener knows what the narrator doesn't)

- Alanis Morissette's "Ironic" is the famous non-example: a textual study located "the irony in 'Ironic'... in the reader and not in the text itself" (Boon, Reading Matrix) — the audience supplies it.
- TV Tropes documents dramatic irony as a common layer of lyrical subtext, including covers that only work if you know the original context.

### The turn

Honest flag: "the turn" is not a strongly attested songwriting term the way "volta" is in poetry. The craft literature describes three concrete pivot devices; treat the turn as this family:

1. **Verse→chorus pivot** (Gary Ewer's checklist: range lift, chord lift, lyric connection, energy moving up).
2. **Bridge as revelation/payoff**, where story and emotion resolve ("Just Give Me a Reason").
3. **Final-chorus reframe**, i.e. Pattison's recoloring, or Springsteen's "Brilliant Disguise," where the repeating line turns from "Is that you, baby, or just a brilliant disguise?" to "Is that me, baby..." — a collapse of mutual recognition, read clinically through Winnicott's "false self" and Lacan's méconnaissance in the British Journal of Psychiatry.

## 6. Music vs lyric irony (lyrical dissonance)

Empirical finding, cross-checked twice: Mori & Iwanaga had 53 students hear happy-music/sad-lyric songs in three conditions; with sad lyrics under happy music, participants **felt as happy as with the music alone**, even while perceiving the combination as sad ("polyvalent pleasant feelings"; a prolactin/calming mechanism hypothesized). The melody transforms the lyric's emotional effect — the music wins.

- "Lyrical dissonance" (TV Tropes) = music and lyrics move in opposite directions; a sub-trope of Mood Dissonance, often comic.
- Canonical cases: "You Are My Sunshine" (1940, bright tune / abandonment lyric), "Copacabana" (saccharine tune / showgirl tragedy), "Mamma Mia", "Lovefool", "Go Your Own Way", "Pumped Up Kicks".
- Advanced move: run dissonance on three levels at once, Lily Allen's "LDN" — perceived-vs-actual reality inside the lyric, melody-vs-lyric between the parts, and the video visualizing the gap.

## 7. Repetition, catchiness, and meaning

Empirical trend (Parada-Cabaleiro et al., 353,320 English lyrics 1970-2020): lyrics got simpler and more repetitive; the repeated-to-non-repeated line ratio nearly doubled in pop; chorus share rose; vocabulary/readability complexity fell; emotion turned more negative and more first-person ("I/me").

Two competing frames, same mechanism:

- **Frame A, repetition as architecture** (Negus & Astor, Cambridge Popular Music): "the art of repetition and play with ambiguity are integral to popular song architectures that endure regardless of the modifications introduced by performers." Their reference base: Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, Middleton's "Play it again, Sam: the productivity of repetition," Margulis's On Repeat.
- **Frame B, repetition as hollow insistence** (Cracked's comic reading): "whenever someone feels the need to repeat something so many times, they're struggling to convince us (or themselves) it's true" — Stardust's "Music Sounds Better With You" repeats its sole refrain 11 times.
- Both agree: repetition shifts a line from information to incantation; it deepens only if the content rewards re-hearing. Catchiness ≠ shallowness ("complex music isn't necessarily better music... we'd all be listening to prog rock"); Missy Elliott's backward lines in "Work It" are clever wordplay automated analysis misreads.

## 8. Canonical song analyses

| Song | Surface | Implied/deeper meaning | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Every Breath You Take" (The Police) | gentle love ballad | surveillance, obsession, control ("you belong to me") | sinister subtext under a comforting melody |
| "Born in the U.S.A." (Springsteen) | patriotic anthem | a Vietnam vet's betrayal and despair | meaning lives in the gap between grim verses and joyous chorus |
| "Pumped Up Kicks" (Foster the People) | cheery whistling indie-pop | a disturbed teen's violent fantasy; a warning about gun violence | upbeat music + dark POV + blank space ("I never say anything about a school") |
| "Losing My Religion" (R.E.M.) | song about faith | obsession / unrequited love / being at the end of one's rope | Southern idiom as title + ambiguous imagery + religious video iconography |
| "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana) | generational anthem | apathy, conformity, "mass mentality" | deliberate ambiguity: deodorant-joke title, "the words are written in code," "that ambiguity or confusion, that's the whole thing" |
| "Brilliant Disguise" (Springsteen) | marriage insecurity | collapse of selfhood, "false self" | a single repeated line turns ("Is that you?" → "Is that me?") |
| "I Follow Rivers" (Lykke Li) | a love-chase | self-destructive attachment, "currents of a dark love" | the title phrase never appears in the lyric; it names the theme, not the text |

Documented conflicts (intent vs reception), each cross-checked:

1. **"Every Breath You Take":** American Songwriter (Paul Zollo) argues the stalker reading is retroactive revisionism; Sting said "I didn't realize at the time how sinister it is." Ultimate Classic Rock and Wikipedia corroborate that Sting intended a romantic song and later recognized and embraced the control/jealousy reading. Resolution: writer's originating emotion and the text's independent meaning are different layers; the song's meaning literally changed ownership.
2. **"Pumped Up Kicks":** Foster denies it's literally about a school shooting ("it's really more about this person's psyche") while the public fixed the school-shooting reading. Unresolved by design: "people fill in the blanks."
3. **"Born in the U.S.A.":** Springsteen puts pride in the chorus, blues in the verses; Reagan-era listeners heard only the chorus. NPR's formulation: the meaning is the distance between verse and chorus.
4. **"Losing My Religion":** apparent sacred/secular clash dissolves on inspection, title is a Southern idiom ("nothing to do with religion"), theme is obsession, imagery is religious. Layered, not contradictory.
5. **Repetition:** architecture (Cambridge) vs decline (Nature/SciAm). Not a contradiction, a tool whose value depends on whether the repeated line rewards re-hearing.

## How to apply this in Suno

- Subtext is written into lyrics, not prompted, so the layering work happens in your `[Verse]/[Chorus]` text. Write the second meaning into concrete nouns/verbs (object-writing output) rather than abstract statements.
- Use Suno's section tags to stage a recoloring motif: keep one hook line identical in `[Chorus 1]`, `[Chorus 2]`, `[Chorus 3]` while verses shift its context; Suno reproduces repeated lines well.
- Exploit lyrical dissonance deliberately: pair upbeat descriptors in the style prompt (`bright uptempo pop, major key`) with dark lyric content; per the research, the melody will still carry the listen, which is the effect you want for irony.
- For unreliable-narrator twist songs, put the tell in the last chorus or bridge and tag it `[Bridge]` or `[Final Chorus]`; Suno's section-repetition (reference 12) helps the turn land as a variation, not a surprise.
- Prompt ambiguity the way Stipe does: avoid over-specifying the lyric scenario in your style prompt so the delivery stays general (`archetypal obsession ballad`, not `song about X specifically`).
- For double entendre, keep the surface register coherent on its own, as in §3; Suno only renders what's in your lyric, so the craft rules above are the whole game here.
- If the meaning depends on a real-world reference (allusion), state it in the style prompt minimally (`Shakespearean imagery`) and let the lyric carry specifics.
