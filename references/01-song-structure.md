# Song Structure: Sections, Forms, and the Energy Curve

How to design a song's skeleton: what each section does, which form fits which genre, how long everything should be, and how to shape tension and release across the whole track.

## Quick rules

- Build everything on a **2/4/8-bar phrase grid**. Change something (add or remove an element) at least every 8 bars, or the ear drifts.
- **ABABCB** (verse chorus verse chorus bridge chorus) is the default pop form. EDM replaces it with intro / build / drop / breakdown / build / drop / outro. Hip-hop is intro / 16-bar verse / 8-bar hook, repeated.
- Get to the chorus in **under 1 minute, ideally under 30 seconds** (industry imperative; contested, see the disagreements section).
- Target length: **3:00 to 3:30** for radio and streaming. A play only counts as a stream after 30 seconds.
- Energy curve: verses dip, choruses peak, the **final chorus is the highest point**. Drop energy after each big chorus so the next one lands harder.
- The pre-chorus is a short rising ramp (2-4 lines, often ending on the V chord). The bridge is a one-off contrast section that recharges the last chorus.
- Post-chorus hooks run about **8 bars** (4 is possible, more than 8 is rare).
- The bar math: at 128 BPM, 1 bar ≈ 2 seconds, so **8 bars ≈ 15 s, 16 bars ≈ 30 s, 32 bars ≈ 1 min**.

## Section-by-section dictionary

| Section | Rule (what it does) | Emotion / function | Concrete example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intro** | Sets atmosphere, groove, and key; may preview chorus or verse chords, or be unrelated; usually ends on a cadence (tonic or dominant). | Suspense, then release on the downbeat; gives the singer a pitch reference. | Marvin Gaye, "Got to Give It Up": party chatter sets the scene before the verse. |
| **Verse** | Same melody, **new lyrics** each time; prolongs the tonic; carries story and detail. Develop the idea, don't restate it (each verse is a new paragraph). | Narrative, conversational, lower energy; sets up the chorus. | "Mr. Brightside" verses; Pat Pattison's "Fathers and Sons" development. |
| **Pre-chorus** (build / channel / lift) | Optional; 2-4 lines over 4-8 bars; raises pitch, intensity, and harmonic tension. Often moves ii-V7 toward the chorus's target chord; ending on the V (5) chord leaves it unresolved, like a coiling spring. | The ramp. Makes the chorus land as release instead of a jump-cut. | "Smells Like Teen Spirit": 8-bar pre-chorus; Lorde, "Royals" ("But every song is like..."). |
| **Chorus** | Repeats music **and** lyrics; always greater intensity than the verse; prolongs the tonic; carries the hook, usually the title. | Peak emotional statement; the song's "mission statement." | Robyn, "Dancing On My Own": the hook "I keep dancing on my own" is the title. |
| **Post-chorus / instrumental hook** | Sits right after the chorus; maintains or raises energy; carries a melodic hook, often vocables ("oh", "ah", "ella-ella"). Typically 8 bars. | Extra hook; extends the party without adding new story. | Ed Sheeran, "Shape of You" ("Oh-I-oh-I..."); Sia, "Chandelier" (detached); Zedd, "Clarity" (pop-drop). |
| **Bridge / middle-8** | One-off section after the second chorus; contrasts melody, harmony, dynamics, and lyrics. 8 bars typical. In AABA form the B is literally the middle eight. | Breaks repetition, offers a new perspective, recharges the final chorus. | John Denver, "Country Roads" (has a bridge) vs Stevie Wonder, "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" (none). |
| **Breakdown** | Drums drop out; chords, melody, and vocal carry. 16-32 bars in EDM. | Breather that makes the next drop feel big. | Standard EDM: intro, build, drop, breakdown, build, drop, outro. |
| **Drop** | Maximum-energy release after a build: full drums, heaviest bass, main hook. 16-32 bars in EDM. | The payoff. The universal tension-then-release principle. | At 128 BPM, 16 bars ≈ 30 seconds. |
| **Instrumental / solo** | Showcases a player over verse, chorus, bridge, or 12-bar chords. Often replaces a third verse. | Contrast plus virtuosity. | "Smells Like Teen Spirit": 8-bar guitar solo at 2:45-3:02. |
| **Outro / coda** | Signals the end; energy dissipates via fade-out, ritardando, or a tag vamp (I-IV, ii-V7). | Closure; the DJ mix-out cue. | "Jingle Bells" outro; tag vamps in soul and gospel. |
| **Ad lib** | Free vocal variation over the coda or outro; may break from the established lyric and melody. | Last burst of intensity; live-version customisation. | End-of-song ad libs in soul, gospel, and pop. |

Cross-checked agreement: three independent sources describe the pre-chorus the same way, a short (2-4 line / 4-8 bar) rising ramp that ends harmonically unresolved (Wikipedia's ii-V7 approach, onloopin's "end on the V chord", and the Puget Sound analysis of Nirvana's 8-bar pre-chorus before a 12-bar chorus).

The Berklee PULSE teaching document lists the common forms as **AB (verse/chorus), verse/chorus/bridge, ABCD (through-composed), 12-bar, 8-bar, 16-bar, and hybrid/compound**, with phrase patterns built from 4- or 8-bar units.

## Song forms and when to use them

| Form | Skeleton | Bars | Genres it suits | Concrete example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AABA / 32-bar** ("American popular song") | A A B A (B = bridge / middle eight) | 4 x 8 = 32 (non-standard 6-6-9-8 exists) | Jazz standards, Great American Songbook, early rock, gospel, ballads | "Over the Rainbow", "I Got Rhythm"; Beatles "Yesterday", "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (note: 12-bar A sections) |
| **Verse-chorus (AB / ABA)** | V C V C... | Verse 8, chorus 8 typical | Rock, pop, country, most modern popular music | "We Are the World" (simple vs contrasting chord progressions) |
| **Verse-pre-chorus-chorus (ABCB)** | V PC C V PC C... | Pre-chorus 4-8 | Anthemic pop/rock where the verse is low-key and the chorus is loud | "Smells Like Teen Spirit" |
| **Verse-chorus-bridge (ABABCB / VCB)** | V C V C B C | Standard pop | Pop, dance, country, Top 40 | "What's Love Got to Do with It", "Hot N Cold", Taylor Swift "Lover" |
| **AAA / strophic** | A A A (same melody, new words) | Varies | Folk, hymns, ballads, blues, story songs | "Amazing Grace"; "Bridge Over Troubled Water" (same verse varied, growing intensity) |
| **Through-composed (ABCD)** | A B C D, nothing repeats | Varies | Art song / lieder, prog, musical theatre, experimental pop | Radiohead "2 + 2 = 5"; Beatles "Happiness Is a Warm Gun" |
| **ABABCBB ("Holy Trinity")** | intro / V / C / V / C / middle-8 / C / chorus-out | - | The dominant 50-year pop format (MusicRadar's name for it); variant ABCABCBC | Per MusicRadar's hit-writing series |
| **EDM (A B C D B C D A)** | Intro / Verse / Build / Drop / Build / Drop / Outro | 8/16/32-bar blocks | House, trance, dubstep, future bass | Short edit = 96 bars (~3 min); club = 192 bars (~6 min) |
| **Hip-hop** | Intro (4-8) / Verse (16) / Hook (8), repeated | Boom-bap verses 16-24; trap verses 8-12 | Rap and its subgenres | Kanye "Gold Digger", Kendrick "Humble", Drake "Nice for What" |

Genre mapping rules (cross-checked across sources):

- **Blues / rock'n'roll: 12-bar and AABA.** The 32-bar form was common in 1950s-60s rock ("Great Balls of Fire", "All I Have to Do Is Dream", "Surfer Girl") before verse-chorus took over.
- **EDM subgenres:** house and tech-house use long steady intros/outros with small 8-bar changes; trance uses longer breakdowns and builds; dubstep and trap-EDM put the first drop inside about 1 minute; future bass and pop-EDM use pop-like verses and choruses with vocal-chop drops.
- **Hip-hop:** trap is hook-heavy and opens on the hook (0:00-0:20) with 8-12-bar verses; boom-bap is verse-first with 16-24-bar verses and the hook arriving 0:40-1:10; drill uses a short verse with the hook at 0:25-0:45; conscious rap may defer the hook past 1:30.
- **Pop vs album:** ABABCB is the default pop form; alternative and progressive acts lean on through-composed, AAA, or mutated AABA.

### Skeleton templates

Standard pop (ABABCB):

```
Intro        4-8 bars   (strip down or preview the hook)
Verse 1      8-16 bars  (new lyric, story setup)
Pre-chorus   4-8 bars   (rising, end unresolved)
Chorus 1     8 bars     (hook + title, peak energy)
Verse 2      8-16 bars  (new angle, not a restatement)
Pre-chorus   4-8 bars
Chorus 2     8 bars
Bridge       8 bars     (contrast; future-facing lyric)
Final chorus 8-16 bars  (highest energy; ad libs allowed)
Outro        4-8 bars   (fade or tag)
```

EDM (A B C D B C D A):

```
A  Intro      8-16 bars  (groove only, filtered)
B  Verse/groove 16 bars
C  Build      8-16 bars  (rise, snare roll, remove elements; hold the kick/bass)
D  Drop       16-32 bars (full drums, heaviest bass, main hook)
C  Build      8-16 bars
D  Drop       16-32 bars
A  Outro      16-32 bars (DJ-friendly, filter down)
```

Do not let the build be bigger than the drop. Hold the kick and bass until the drop hits.

Hip-hop:

```
Intro        4-8 bars
Verse 1      16 bars    (boom-bap 16-24; trap 8-12)
Hook         8 bars     (trap hooks may run 8-16)
Verse 2      16 bars
Hook         8 bars
(optional Bridge) 8 bars
Hook / Outro 8-16 bars
```

Total: roughly 60-72 bars, which lands around 3:00-4:30.

## Length norms

| Claim | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Billboard Hot 100 average (historical) | ~3:30 | musicianwave (Billboard-derived) |
| Hot 100 peak to decline | 4:39 in 1992 fell ~31% to 3:13 by 2019 | metamusicmedia (cites Billboard) |
| 2025 top-10 hits lasting 10+ weeks | 3:17 average | metamusicmedia |
| Radio-ready length | 3:00 plus or minus 0:30; hook within the first 30 s; keep instrumental breaks short | Music Industry HowTo |
| Time to reach the chorus | Under 1 min, ideally under 30 s | MusicRadar |
| Streaming payout threshold | A play counts as a stream after 30 seconds | metamusicmedia |
| Genre exceptions | Punk under 3 min; prog 5-7 min; techno/house 6-8 min; jazz ~8 min; drone 10+ min | musicianwave |
| EDM edits | Short ~3 min (96 bars) for radio; club 5-7 min (192 bars) | Mix Elite |
| Rap | 60-72 bars ≈ 3:00-4:30 | eMastered Rap |

Where the extra seconds hide: (1) an intro that takes 16 bars to reach a vocal, (2) a second verse repeating the first verse's job, (3) an outro riding the beat after the last line. Fixes: cut the intro to 4 bars, give verse 2 a new angle or trim it to 8 bars, end on the last hook.

The counter-argument, kept rather than averaged: RIFF STREET argues the chorus-within-30-seconds rule suffocates slow-burn songs. Tracks like Oasis "Champagne Supernova" (chorus after 2:30) and Portishead "Roads" (a 2-minute build) would struggle under the rule, and the album opener has been reshaped into a make-or-break track.

## The energy curve

The canonical pop rule: energy dips for verses, peaks for choruses, and hits its highest point at the final chorus. Release energy after each big chorus so the next one hits harder. EDM replaces the final chorus with a second drop and inserts a breakdown where pop would put a bridge.

Text version of the map (energy on a 1-10 scale):

```
lowest ->                                                        -> highest ->  -> low
INTRO    VERSE    PRE-CHORUS    CHORUS   [dip] VERSE   PRE-CHORUS   CHORUS   BRIDGE/BREAKDOWN   FINAL CHORUS   OUTRO
(2-4)    (4-5)    (6-7)         (9-10)         (4-5)   (6-7)        (9-10)   (3-5, contrast)    (10, peak)     (2-3, fade)
bars:    4-8      8             8              8-16    4-8          8        8 (16-32 EDM)      8-16           4-8
```

Levers to RAISE energy: add instruments or layers; move higher in register; increase density (more and faster notes, shorter chords); get louder; add harmonic tension (sus chords, unresolved harmonies); increase tempo feel (double-time hats, syncopation).

Levers to RELEASE energy: remove instruments; drop the drums; get quieter; simplify (fewer notes, longer chords, space).

Concrete tension techniques (each backed by two or more sources):

- **Strategic silence / drop-out before the chorus.** Drop everything for one beat, or remove the kick for the last bar of a build.
- **The pre-chorus lift.** Rising pitch plus an unresolved V chord plus a drum fill or stripped arrangement.
- **Harmonic tension.** A sus4 waiting to resolve; G7 into C; a deceptive cadence (G into Am); a borrowed Fm in C major; chromatic passing chords.
- **Bridge contrast.** To exceed the chorus high point: add a new or borrowed chord (flat-3, flat-7, or 2 major), more frequent chord changes, wider intervals, anticipate downbeats. To lower it: fewer chord changes, stay on one chord, drop the vocal register, add rests.
- **Tempo automation / rhythmic displacement.** Drop about 5 BPM over 4 bars then snap back; shift a groove an eighth late for two bars.
- **Less is more at scale.** Modern pop uses more sonic variety than musical variety; filters let a loop repeat without boredom.
- **Dynamic range is genre-dependent.** Wide range suits orchestral, ballad, and acoustic styles; narrow range suits EDM, punk, and heavy rock.

Best-documented real energy arc (Berklee Today's analysis of Paul Simon, "Bridge Over Troubled Water"): piano alone for the first ~1:30 (intro, verse 1, turnaround, verse 2); vibes add shimmer at the top of verse 2; strings and a processed snare enter at bar 64; bass at bar 66; drums only in the last third; the vocal and arrangement peak at bars 89-91 (Garfunkel's A-flat); the piano thins out and passes to strings and percussion for the coda. The write-up calls it "ultimate restraint, building slowly and deliberately from beginning to end."

Tension-before-payoff rule: the payoff must arrive after tension is built. Front-loading the catchiest bit collapses the structure.

## Chorus and hook anatomy

1. **The chorus is the mission statement.** It repeats music and lyrics, always runs at higher intensity, contains the hook, and usually states the title.
2. **Title rhythm = melody rhythm.** The rhythm of the title words creates the rhythm of the melodic hook; melody and words are born as one. Example: Lenny Kravitz, "American Woman": two neighbouring tones, the rhythm does the work.
3. **Hook is not chorus.** A hook is a short, flexible, highly repeatable phrase that can appear in the intro, verse, bridge, or outro. A chorus is a longer, positioned section that contains the hook.
4. **Generate volume, then filter.** Andrea Stolpe: write 10 times more titles than you'll develop, and 10 times more songs than you'll record, because "your instincts will better know a great hook when you hear one."
5. **Verses control the angle of entry.** They get us ready to hear the chorus. Stagnant verses make the repeated chorus stagnant too.
6. **Verses must develop, not restate.** Pattison's sheriff demo: verse 1, the toughest man in town; verse 2, he's obsessed with a woman; verse 3, she's married to the weakest man. That progression creates curiosity; restating the same words creates boredom.
7. **The bridge sets up the last chorus.** It contrasts dynamics and lyrics, looks to the future ("Someday...", "Maybe when...", "I used to be... now I'm finally..."), and recharges the final chorus.
8. **Memorability can be engineered.** Hooktheory's SongMetrics levers: chord-melody tension (melody notes outside the chord), chord-progression novelty, melodic complexity (non-diatonic notes plus syncopation), and chord-bass melody (stepwise bass). Craft melodies that build and release tension at the right moments.
9. **A hook can be non-lyrical.** Post-chorus hooks often run on vocables ("oh", "ah", "ella-ella") while hammering the title; "Shape of You"'s 8-bar post-chorus has only three lines of actual lyric.

Worked example cited by two sources: Robyn, "Dancing On My Own". The hook "I keep dancing on my own" sits at the end of the chorus and is also the title; the chorus shifts chord structure at 0:50-1:25.

## Bar lengths and pacing

| Section | Pop/rock bars | EDM bars | Rap bars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro | 4-8 | 8-16 (8 or fewer for radio edits) | 4-8 |
| Verse | 8-16 | 16 (verse 1) / 16-32 (verse 2) | 16 (boom-bap 16-24; trap 8-12) |
| Pre-chorus | 4-8 (2-4 lines) | - | 4 |
| Chorus / hook | 8 | 16 (drop) | 8 (trap 8-16) |
| Post-chorus | 8 (4 possible; more than 8 is rare) | - | - |
| Bridge / middle-8 | 8 (the B of AABA) | breakdown 16-32 | 8 |
| Build-up | - | 8-16 | - |
| Outro | 4-8 | 16-32 | 4-8 |

Pacing rules:

- Change something at least every 8 bars (add or remove one element), or the ear drifts.
- A 32-bar intro is great for a DJ and too slow for a playlist. Decide the audience first.
- Pattison's prosody: match form to meaning. Short phrases with dense, consecutive rhymes read as "built for speed"; long phrases with spread rhymes read as slow. Form follows function.
- The 16-bar verse is a convention, not a law. Deviation is itself expressive: Kendrick's 12-bar verses feel compressed; Aesop Rock's 24-bar verses feel relentless.

## Where sources disagree (uncertainty flags preserved)

1. **"Chorus" is historically ambiguous.** In 1920s-40s usage the entire 32-bar AABA was called "the chorus" and the opening section "the verse", the opposite of modern usage.
2. **Post-chorus vs the second half of a two-part chorus** is subjective. Jason Blume's criteria (few new lyrics, hammers the title, simple hook) are explicitly "not cut-and-dried".
3. **Section labeling is contested** even among theorists (pre-chorus vs "first chorus" vs "verse part b"). Asaf Peres argues a complete analysis separates the songwriting dimension from the production dimension: a section can be a chorus by songwriting and a build by production.
4. **Length averages conflict by dataset and year:** ~3:30 (musicianwave, historical Hot 100) vs 3:17 (2025 top-10s) vs a "3:00 sweet spot". Different samples, not a contradiction.
5. **AABA section length is not fixed at 8.** Ethan Hein documents 12-bar A sections ("I Want to Hold Your Hand") and odd lengths ("She Said She Said"); Songstuff lists "Send in the Clowns" as 6-6-9-8.
6. **Source error to discount:** eMastered's structure article labels a section "12-Bar Blues or AABA", conflating two distinct forms. Wikipedia and the Puget Sound textbook treat them separately. Treat the pairing as an editorial slip.
7. **The 30-second rule is contested.** MusicRadar states it as an industry imperative; RIFF STREET argues it damages slow-burn songwriting. Both positions are included above.

## How to apply this in Suno

- Write the form directly into the lyrics with bracket tags ([Intro], [Verse], [Pre-Chorus], [Chorus], [Post-Chorus], [Bridge], [Outro]) and control section length through line count: 2-4 lines for a pre-chorus, 4-8 for a verse, a short repeated block for the hook.
- Name the form in the style prompt when it matters: "verse pre-chorus chorus bridge pop", "AABA jazz standard", "verse hook verse hook rap, 16-bar verses".
- Put the energy curve in as arrangement words per section: "stripped piano and vocal verse", "full drums and stacked vocals chorus", "breakdown bridge, drums drop out", "final chorus, key change energy peak" only if you actually want that lift.
- State the tempo and the bar math when pacing matters: "128 BPM, 8-bar intro", "16-bar verse, 8-bar hook".
- Ask for the post-chorus explicitly if you want the extra hook: "[Post-Chorus] (oh-i-oh-i)" after each chorus.
- Keep the section count lean when you want a radio-shaped 3:00 song: no 32-bar intros, no repeated second verse, end on the last hook rather than a long outro.
- For slow-burn songs, front-load atmosphere instead: long intro, low first verse, delayed chorus, and say so in the style prompt so the model doesn't rush to the hook.
