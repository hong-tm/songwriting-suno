# Lyric Craft: Prosody, Imagery, Rhyme, and Singability

How to write words a singer can actually sing: the writing process (title and central idea first), the jobs of verse, chorus, and bridge, show-don't-tell, stress-to-beat matching, line length and meter, the full rhyme taxonomy and schemes, internal and multisyllabic rhyme, metaphor, point of view, and the classic pitfalls.

## Quick rules

- Decide the **one central idea** (usually the title) first, then prove it. Verses tell the story; the chorus is the thesis that hammers the title.
- **Show, don't tell:** use Action, Imagery, Detail. Establish time and place early. Never state an emotion the listener can infer from a scene.
- **Match lexical stress to the beat.** Stressed syllables are sung higher, louder, longer, and land on strong beats. A mismatch is a "mis-stressed" lyric.
- Keep **line lengths consistent** within a section. The classic template is common meter: 8.6.8.6 syllables, iambic tetrameter alternating with trimeter.
- Rhyme is an expressive dial: **perfect rhymes read as stable and resolved; assonance rhymes read as unstable.** Pick rhyme by the emotional job of the line.
- Verses most often run **AABB or ABAB**; ballad meter runs **ABCB**.
- Put **open vowels** (ah, oh) under long sustained notes rather than closed ones.
- **Write lyrics, not poetry.** A lyric serves the music; don't overwrite.

| Step | What to do | Source |

| Who / When / Where drills | Lessons 2-4 pair idea generation with perspective: "'Who' Writing", "'When' Writing", "'Where' Writing" | same |
| Build outward from the concrete | Establish time and location, then add Action, Imagery, Detail; generalize only in the chorus | BMI / Jason Blume |

Berklee's Lyric Writing: Tools and Strategies syllabus names the rest of the toolkit: object writing, balancing and unbalancing the number of lines, line length, the rhyme families, the prosody of rhyme, metaphor, common meter and couplets, rhyme structure, and types of closure.

## Verse = story, chorus = thesis, bridge = turn

Flow (text version):

```
Verse   → details: the story, the events, images, emotions. New lyrics each time.
Chorus  → main idea + hook. Highest intensity. General summation; hammers the title.
Bridge  → words and music pattern change. Usually ends on the dominant. Sets up the return.
Chorus  → payoff. The bridge's reframe makes the hook land harder.
```

- Wikipedia's song structure entry: "the verse contains the details of the song: the story, the events, images and emotions", each verse with different lyrics; the chorus carries the main idea at higher intensity; the bridge is where "the pattern of the words and music change" and typically ends on the dominant as a re-transition (the "middle eight").
- BMI/Blume: the chorus should be a general summation of the concept that hammers home the title. Telling the story is the domain of the verses.
- Billboard's study of the century's best bridges: a bridge appears after at least one chorus, introduces new musical and thematic elements, then returns to the refrain. The best ones are "storytelling bridges": bringing in something that hasn't been said before, the most revealing part of the song.
- Refrain: a repeated line or phrase; in verse-refrain (AAA) forms the refrain is the line that communicates the central idea and often serves as the title.

### Lyric skeleton template

```
[Verse 1]
Line 1  Where and when: establish time and place             (A)
Line 2  What happened: action + one concrete image           (A or B)
Line 3  What happened next: sensory detail, specific nouns   (A)
Line 4  A phrase that leans toward the chorus's idea         (B)

[Chorus]
Line 1  Title line: the central idea, plain and singable     (A)
Line 2  Support line: more general, the emotional thesis     (B)
Line 3  Title line again, or a small variation               (A)
Line 4  Payoff line that rhymes back or re-hammers the title (B)

[Bridge]
Line 1  New angle or consequence not yet stated              (X)
Line 2  The reframe that makes the final chorus hit harder   (Y)
```

## Show, don't tell: Action, Imagery, Detail

Blume's three steps, in order: (1) **Action**, (2) **Imagery**, (3) **Detail**. Establish time and location so the listener stands somewhere. State nothing the scene can imply: don't announce "I was sad", show the cold coffee, the unanswered phone, the coat still on the hook. Verses carry "the images and emotions the writer wishes to express"; the chorus is where the feeling gets named, if it gets named at all. Point of view choices (below) change how immediate the imagery feels.

## Prosody: fitting words to music

- **Stress alignment.** Words carry stressed and unstressed syllables; a well-set lyric aligns the word's stress with the music's beats. A setting that fights the word is "mis-stressed" ("to-DAY" sung as "TO-day").
- **Pattison's prosody.** Prosody is the way melody, harmony, rhythm, and lyric all work together to support the song's central message (the term is anchored in Writing Better Lyrics, pp. 179-180, and appears in Berklee Press songwriting curricula).
- **What stress means in singing:** stressed syllables are higher in pitch, louder, and longer, and they land on the beat.
- **Line length.** Keep lengths consistent inside a section; Berklee teaches "Length of Lines, and Common Meter" and balancing vs. unbalancing the number of lines between sections.
- **Common meter.** 8.6.8.6 syllables, iambic tetrameter alternating with trimeter; ballad meter rhymes ABCB. Because thousands of texts share the pattern, one song's lyrics fit another's tune (Amazing Grace / House of the Rising Sun / Advance Australia Fair). That portability is the strongest sourced evidence that matched stress patterns are what make lines singable.
- **Contrast.** Pattison's "form follows function": change line length, rhyme density, or phrasing between sections so the form mirrors the meaning. Short phrases with dense consecutive rhymes read fast; long phrases with spread rhymes read slow.
- **Open vowels.** An open (low) vowel, with the tongue far from the roof of the mouth (like "ah"), is the practical choice under long sustained notes. Flag: this is vocal-practice consensus, asserted but not confirmed by a high-tier fetched source.
- **Verse vs. chorus rhythm.** Verses take shorter notes and more syncopation; choruses take longer notes with less syncopation.

## Rhyme: the full taxonomy

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Perfect (full/exact)** | Stressed vowel + all following sounds identical; the onset differs | sight/flight, deign/gain, love/dove |
| Masculine (single) | Perfect rhyme stressed on the final syllable | rhyme/sublime |
| Feminine (double) | Stress on the penultimate syllable | picky/tricky |
| Dactylic | Stress on the antepenultimate syllable | amorous/glamorous |
| Compound (mosaic) | A multi-word match | poet / know it |
| **Imperfect / near / slant / half** | Similar but not identical; vowels differ with consonants matching, or the reverse | hand/lend, begun/afternoon, queen/regime |
| **Assonance** | Matching vowel sounds only | lean green meat; shake/hate |
| **Consonance** | Matching consonants, vowels differ | rabies/robbers, coming/home |
| **Pararhyme** | All consonants match | tick/tock |
| **Alliteration (head rhyme)** | Matching initial consonants | ship/short |
| **Identical rhyme** | Same vowel *and* same onset; considered inferior in English | gun/begun, bare/bear |
| **Eye rhyme** | Same spelling, different sound | cough/bough, love/move |
| **Forced / oblique** | An imperfect sound match strained for the sake of rhyme | green/fiend, one/thumb |
| **Semirhyme** | One word carries an extra syllable | bend/ending |
| **Internal** | Rhyme inside a line, or between internal phrases | see below |
| **Multisyllabic** | Whole phrases rhyme | see below |

Perfect rhyme's precise condition: the stressed vowel is identical, everything after it is identical, and the onset consonant differs. Rap leans on slant rhyme to escape cliché and to chain longer multisyllabic matches.

## Rhyme schemes and the prosody of rhyme

| Scheme | Where it shows up | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| AABB | Verse couplets; the most common verse pattern per Wikipedia | Strong closure on every second line |
| ABAB | Alternating verse rhyme; equally common | Delays closure, spreads the rhyme |
| ABCB | Ballad meter (with common/ballad meter 8.6.8.6) | Only the 2nd and 4th lines rhyme; the most portable pattern |
| AAAA | Monorhyme stanzas | Maximum stability, risk of monotony |
| Cross/internal chains | Rap and hip-hop | Complex, often multisyllabic, often mid-line |

- **Stability mapping (use rhyme as an expressive dial):** perfect rhyme (fire/desire) and family rhyme (mud/truck) read as stable and resolved; assonance rhyme (life/tide) reads as unstable, because the vowel matches but the consonants don't resolve. Berklee teaches this as the "prosody of rhyme" and "types of closure": a perfect rhyme closes a section; an assonance rhyme leaves it open.
- Pattison's system also names **family, additive, and subtractive** rhyme as graded steps between perfect and assonance. The category names are documented in Berklee's syllabus; the definitions live in Pattison's The Essential Guide to Rhyming, which was not retrievable in this research pass. Do not guess the definitions; treat them as the intermediate rungs on the stability scale.
- Rhyme placement is structure: which lines rhyme, and how tightly, is part of how a section opens or closes.

## Internal and multisyllabic rhyme

- **Internal rhyme** sits inside a line or between internal phrases: "The guests are met, the feast is set"; Dylan: "Once upon a time you dressed so fine / threw the bums a dime in your prime".
- **Multisyllabic rhyme** matches whole phrases: AZ, "young and gifted / my tongue's prolific". Rap builds long chains of these out of slant matches.

## Metaphor

Berklee gives metaphor multiple dedicated lessons ("Working with Metaphors, Delaying the Tonic"; "Metaphors, Second-Person Narrative, Rhyme Types"). Use metaphor to say the thing without naming it, and pair it with a deliberate POV so the image has a body.

## Point of view, tense, and address

- **First person (I):** confessional, immediate.
- **Third person (he/she/they):** narrative distance, observer.
- **Second person (you):** direct address; imperative lines can drop "you" entirely ("Come back").
- **Present tense** buys immediacy; past tense tells.
- Berklee pairs POV with craft drills: "Who Writing" (lesson 2), "Where Writing, First-Person Narrative" (lesson 4), "Second-Person Narrative" (lesson 6). Keep one POV per section; shifting POV mid-song is a meaning choice, not an accident.

## Common pitfalls (with fixes)

| Pitfall | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Telling the emotion | The listener is told, not moved | Show it: Action, Imagery, Detail (BMI/Blume) |
| Mis-stressed lyrics | Word stress fights the beat | Say the line aloud, mark stresses, align them to downbeats (Lyric setting) |
| Identical rhyme | Same onset makes it feel lazy in English | Vary the onset, or use it deliberately as rime riche |
| Clichéd rhymes (knowledge/college) | Obvious and boring | Use slant/assonance or multisyllabic rhymes instead |
| Overwriting / writing a poem | Doesn't serve the music | Write as a lyricist: shorter, singable, repeatable |
| Relying on eye rhymes | Spelling matches, sound doesn't (cough/bough) | Rhyme by sound, never by spelling |
| Forced rhymes (green/fiend) | The sense bends to serve the rhyme | Change the line, not the meaning |
| Varying syllable counts between matching sections | The repeated melody won't fit verse 2 | Keep parallel sections syllable-parallel; rewrite rather than pad |

## Prosody and process drills

```
Object writing drill (Pattison/Berklee, ~10 minutes)
1. Pick one concrete noun as the prompt (a coffee cup, a bus stop).
2. Write nonstop through every sense: sight, sound, smell, taste,
   touch, body sensation, movement.
3. Do not edit. Keep the raw material.
4. Harvest images and phrases from it when drafting verses.

Stress check per line (before you commit a lyric)
1. Say the line aloud at conversation speed.
2. Mark the syllables that naturally receive stress.
3. Confirm each marked syllable can land on a strong beat, and that
   the line's key words can take the melody's highest, longest notes.
4. Rewrite any word whose stress fights the beat.

Line-length check
1. Count syllables per line within a section.
2. Match counts across parallel sections (verse 1 vs. verse 2).
3. For a classic shape, target common meter: 8.6.8.6 with ABCB.
```

## Where sources disagree (uncertainty flags preserved)

1. **Chorus vs. refrain.** One convention reserves "refrain" for a repeated line inside a section and "chorus" for a discrete repeated section (von Appen & Frei-Hauenschild); most pop writing uses the terms loosely.
2. **Common meter's rhyme scheme.** The ABAB claim in Wikipedia's common-meter article is marked citation-needed; ballad meter ABCB is the well-attested pattern. Treat ABAB as conventional wisdom, ABCB as documented.
3. **"Assonance = slant rhyme."** Loose usage exists; the strict taxonomy separates assonance (vowels only), consonance (consonants only), and half rhyme (final consonants). Slant rhyme is best treated as the umbrella.
4. **Identical rhyme.** Called inferior in English yet prized as rime riche in French tradition. Context-dependent, not wrong.
5. **Family / additive / subtractive rhyme definitions.** Named in Berklee's syllabus, defined only in Pattison's book, which was not fetchable. The names are certain; the definitions were not verified here.
6. **Open-vowel singability.** Vocal-practice consensus that open vowels sustain and project better on long notes; no high-tier fetched source states it explicitly. Use it as craft guidance, not settled fact.
7. **Title-first formalization.** Berklee's refrain/central-idea teaching and Pattison's title-driven process support it; Sheila Davis's classic title-first method is cited in the literature but the book text was not retrievable.

## How to apply this in Suno

- Suno sings your text literally: run the stress check before pasting. A line that mis-stresses on paper will mis-stress in the render; rewrite rather than hope.
- Put the title in the chorus (first or last line is safest) and repeat it. Repetition is how Suno finds the hook; give the hook the simplest wording in the song.
- Keep parallel sections syllable-parallel (verse 1 vs. verse 2, chorus 1 vs. chorus 2) so the generated melody repeats cleanly.
- Feed concrete sensory nouns and verbs instead of emotion labels; abstract "I feel" lines tend to render generic.
- End chorus lines on open vowels (go, so, now, high, stay) where you want big sustained notes; end verse lines on whatever consonants the story needs.
- Choose rhyme by section job: perfect rhymes for the chorus's closure, assonance or slant rhymes in a bridge or pre-chorus you want to feel unresolved.
- Use bracket tags ([Verse], [Chorus], [Bridge]) and keep your written scheme intact inside each; see `11-suno-prompting.md` for tag syntax and `13-vocal-control.md` for delivery control.
- Machine-check before you ship: `python3 scripts/lyric_lint.py <lyric-file>` prints the per-section rhyme scheme, near-rhymes (assonance), repeated lines, line-length spread, and hits against a built-in cliché list. It is an orthographic approximation (no pronunciation dictionary) — an aid for the eye, not a verdict.
