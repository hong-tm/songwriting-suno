# Rhythm, Tempo, Meter, and Groove

How to choose a tempo, pick a meter, program a genre-identifying drum pattern, and make a groove feel a specific mood. Includes both vendor swing settings and measured performance research.

## Quick rules

- **The backbeat decides perceived tempo, not the hi-hat rate.** Snare on 2 and 4 = normal time; snare on 3 = half-time (a 140 BPM trap beat feels like 70). Hi-hats keep running at full speed either way.
- Pick BPM from the genre table first: hip-hop 70-100, pop 100-130, house 115-130, dubstep/trap/drill 135-150, DnB 160-180 (174 is canonical).
- **Syncopation is not swing.** Syncopation accents unexpected grid cells; swing warps the grid itself. Latin music is heavy on syncopation and light on swing; old-time country is the reverse.
- Swing settings on the MPC scale: **50% straight, 54-58% classic hip-hop, 66% perfect triplet, 75% maximum** (beyond 75% it stops reading as swing). Measured jazz data says the real ratio is tempo-dependent: about 1:1 at fast tempos, up to ~3.5:1 at slow ones.
- Count odd meters in subgroups, and **4+3 is musically different from 3+4**. Counting against the music makes the groove fight itself.
- **Stressed syllables go on strong beats.** Don't give insignificant syllables long notes, the downbeat, or accents.
- Rhythm is the genre identity. The same pitch content at 95 vs 105 BPM shifts R&B into pop; four-on-the-floor means house, snare-on-3 means trap or dubstep, one-drop means reggae.
- Avoid random timing variation. Roger Linn: if dynamics and swing are right, the groove works best when notes land exactly on their time slots.

## Tempo and emotion

| BPM band | Physical / emotional response | Idiomatic use |
|---|---|---|
| Under 80 | Sway rather than nod; calm, sadness, intimacy | Ballads, slow jams, ambient |
| 80-100 | Head-nod; relaxed but grooving | Hip-hop, R&B, reggae |
| 100-120 | "Walking pace"; upbeat without frantic | Pop, funk, mid-tempo rock |
| 120-130 | Dance floor centre; matches an elevated heart rate; easiest DJ mixing | House, dance-pop |
| 130-150 | High energy; half-time feels common | Techno, drill, uptempo EDM |
| Over 150 | Intensity through sheer speed; physically tiring | DnB, punk, fast metal |

Supporting research: music over 120 BPM can increase stress and attention lapses while driving (NIH/PMC6636224); faster tempo reads as energized and excited, slower as calm or sad.

## Time signatures

| Signature | Type | Beat feel | Idiomatic use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4/4 | Simple quadruple | **1** 2 3 4 | Default: rock, blues, country, funk, pop |
| 2/2 (cut, alla breve) | Simple duple | 2 beats | Marches, fast orchestral |
| 2/4 | Simple duple | 2 beats | Polka, galop, march; bolero, cumbia, merengue |
| 3/4 | Simple triple | **1** 2 3 | Waltz, minuet, scherzo, mazurka, country ballad |
| 3/8 | Simple triple | Faster/shorter hypermeter | Some folk dances (cachucha) |
| 6/8 | Compound duple | **1**23 **4**56 (stress on 1 and 4) | Jigs, tarantella, marches, "We Are the Champions" |
| 9/8 | Compound triple | 3 x 3 | Slip jigs; "Blue Rondo a la Turk" = 2+2+2+3 |
| 12/8 | Compound quadruple | 4 x 3 | Shuffle/blues, doo-wop |
| 5/4 | Odd | Grouped 3+2 or 2+3 | "Take Five", Mission: Impossible, Holst "Mars", Tchaikovsky Pathetique |
| 7/8 | Odd | 4+3 / 3+4 / 2+2+3 | Rush "Tom Sawyer" synth break; Zeppelin "The Ocean" (4/4 alternating with 7/8) |
| 7/4 | Odd | 4+3 or 3+4 | Peter Gabriel "Solsbury Hill" (riff 3+4, verse 4+3), Donny Hathaway "This Christmas" |
| 11/8 | Odd | Truncated 12/8 | Allman Brothers "Whipping Post" |

Rules:

- Simple meter = the beat divides in 2 (2/4, 3/4, 4/4). Compound meter = the beat divides in 3 (6/8, 9/8, 12/8).
- **3/4 vs 6/8:** both hold six eighth notes; the difference is grouping, not speed. 3/4 = three quarter-note beats, eighths beamed in pairs (waltz). 6/8 = two dotted-quarter beats, eighths beamed in threes, stress on 1 and 4 (jig). Switching between those groupings is hemiola ("America" from West Side Story).
- Odd meters are counted in subgroups, and [4+3] differs musically from [3+4]. Takadimi syllables work well for tricky counts.

## Half-time and double-time feel

- **Common time:** bass drum on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, eighth-note hats.
- **Half-time:** the backbeat moves to **beat 3** (beats 3 and 7 of an 8-beat span); hats play quarters. Perceived tempo is roughly halved. Examples: the Purdie Shuffle (Steely Dan "Home at Last", "Babylon Sisters"), Led Zeppelin "Fool in the Rain", the Rosanna shuffle (Toto).
- **Double-time:** snare moves to the "&" beats, hats subdivide sixteenths. Common in jazz solos.

The BPM illusion: a trap beat at 140 puts the kick on 1 and the snare on 3, so it feels like 70 BPM while the hats run at 140+. DnB at 174 = 87 BPM half-count; 174 and 87 describe the same recording (about 345 ms per beat). The snare's position sets the felt tempo.

## Groove vocabulary and rules

- **Downbeat** = beat 1 of the bar. **Upbeat** = strictly the last beat before a downbeat (pickup / anacrusis); in practice, universally, the "&" offbeats. **On-beat** = pulses 1-2-3-4; **off-beat** = the "&"s.
- **Backbeat** = accent on beats **2 and 4** in 4/4, normally snare, claps, or stacked cymbals. Foundational to R&B, rock, soul, funk, reggae, and hip-hop. The backbeat is so entrenched that syncopation now usually means *displacing* it.
- **Syncopation** = accenting weak beats or subdivisions. Strength ladder (clock-face model): downbeat strongest, then beat 3 (almost a second downbeat), then beats 2 and 4, then eighth offbeats, then sixteenth offbeats (weakest; where James Brown funk lives).
- **Tresillo** = a 3+3+2 grouping of subdivisions; the first half of 3-2 son clave; ubiquitous in current pop.
- **Pocket** = everyone shares the same groove and micro-timing; a held "deep pocket" never wavers. Hip-hop's equivalent of swing is **flow**.
- **Groove** has two competing definitions: the unpredictable microrhythms performers create when deviating from the grid (Hein), versus the "aesthetics of exactitude" finding (Fruhauf, Kopiez, Platz 2013) that the strongest groove response comes from drum patterns with **no** microtiming deviations. See the measured-research section below.
- **Delayed/late backbeat:** snare slightly late with the following gap shortened so tempo doesn't drag. Common in late-60s/70s rock and soul ("Green Onions", "Hey Joe", Charlie Watts) and in the Funky Drummer break. **Early backbeat:** Springsteen "The River", Police "Every Breath You Take", D'Angelo "The Line".

## Swing and shuffle: the numbers

Vendor settings (MPC/Reason scale, for an eighth-note pair or the delay applied to even 16ths):

| Setting | Effect |
|---|---|
| 50% | Straight, equal 16ths, no swing |
| 54% | Loosens a straight 16th beat "without it sounding like swing" (Roger Linn) |
| 54-58% | Classic MPC swing range for hip-hop |
| 55% | Light shuffle (Reason Redrum) |
| ~62% | Looser than triplet; Reason default Global Shuffle, "almost triplet" |
| 65-66% | Perfect triplet swing: first 16th takes 2/3, second takes 1/3 |
| 75% | Maximum MPC swing / extreme shuffle; beyond this it no longer reads as swing |

Other ratios: **shuffle / 12-8 feel = strict 2:1** (Grateful Dead "Truckin'"); **quintuplet swing = 3:2**; **septuplet swing = 4:3** (both read as "drunken"). Note the definitions: Hein insists swing is not merely tied triplets; 2:1 is one ratio on a continuum, and Linn treats 66% as a reference point, so the two views are compatible.

Era shift: **eighth-note swing** dominates pre-1960 jazz and 1950s rock; **sixteenth-note swing** dominates post-1960 funk, hip-hop, and reggae. Black American music's basic pulse unit moved from 8ths to 16ths around 1960. Stevie Wonder "Superstition" and Jackson 5 "I Want You Back" carry heavy 16th swing; "Rockin' Robin" is 8th swing. Funk-style swung 16ths use placements around 0/40/50/80% versus triplet-based 0/33/50/75%.

Swing is not static: heavier at slow tempos, lighter at fast; it can change within a phrase; different instruments can run different ratios at once (Harvey Mason on "Chameleon": heavier swing on kick/snare, lighter on hi-hat). In notation you don't spell swing out; you write straight and mark "swing" at the top. Most notation software supports only eighth-note swing (Noteflight and Dorico also do 16ths).

### Measured performance research (corrects the vendor picture)

- **Swing ratio is tempo-dependent, not a constant 2:1.** Friberg & Sundstrom measured the ratio approaching **1:1 at fast tempos** and rising to **almost 3.5:1 at slow tempos** (the triplet-feel belief was not confirmed). In practice jazz sits between **1:1 and 3:1** (Datseris et al. 2019). Swing ratio decreases as tempo increases.
- **The short note stays ~100 ms** at medium-to-fast tempos, the perceptual floor for hearing a tone as an individual event. At very slow tempos the floor breaks down (DeJohnette excerpt at 72 BPM had a 416 ms short note). Listeners' preferred ratio follows the same tempo curve.
- **Microtiming deviations are small and probably not intentional:** sigma ≈ 18.5 ± 2 ms, normally distributed, constant across tempos (Datseris et al. 2019). Quantized versions of performances were rated slightly **higher** on swing; doubling the deviations rated lower; inverting them made no difference.
- **Does microtiming cause groove?** The literature lands on an inverted U near zero: groove ratings stay high for deviations at or below the original performance and drop when exaggerated; fully quantized and original rate about equally (Senn et al. 2016; Fruhauf et al. 2013). Experts are more sensitive than non-experts. Body-movement data adds nuance: tight-but-non-zero timing produced the largest entrainment (Kilchenmann & Senn 2015).

### Dilla time (quantified)

Straight time is metronomic; swing time stretches each beat's halves; **Dilla time runs multiple feels at once**, some straight, some swung, some ahead, some behind. The deviations were deliberate and meticulously executed, not sloppiness. Because the same microtimings repeat every bar, repetition magnifies the effect (unlike jazz, where the time feel is organic and never identical bar to bar).

Concrete moves: in Slum Village "Get Dis Money" the eighth notes are near-straight but the backbeat claps land slightly **early**, making everything else feel like it drags, and the hi-hats on 1.1.3 and 1.3.3 are **late**. Reproduction method: move the snare a 32nd note early, and/or juxtapose straight and swung 16th feels; Dilla also slowed samples to exaggerate their existing deviations.

## Note values, subdivisions, and lyric prosody

Counting grid:

```
quarter:    1        2        3        4
eighth:     1  &     2  &     3  &     4  &
sixteenth:  1 e & a   2 e & a   3 e & a   4 e & a
```

Pop rarely uses values below 16ths except as un-notatable microrhythm.

Prosody rules (Berklee / Pat Pattison):

- Prosody means every song element supports the central message.
- **Core rule:** stressed syllables and important words fall on strong beats; insignificant syllables should not get long notes, the beat itself, or accentuation. Musical phrases should align with grammatical phrases so pauses land where a speaker would pause.
- Ableton's cross-check states the inverse trap: weakly stressed syllables landing on strong beats and long durations, with strongly accented syllables short, reads wrong.
- **Stable vs unstable:** decide whether a lyric idea is stable or unstable, then match melody, harmony, melodic rhythm, and harmonic rhythm. Perfect and family rhymes support stable, resolved meaning; assonance supports unstable, unresolved meaning.
- Constructional elements to align with meaning: tempo, harmonic rhythm, chord quality, key, arrangement, lyric density, melodic note values, melodic contour, rhythmic feel.
- Practical notation: underline stressed words, lay lyrics on a "1 e & a" grid, use Takadimi for odd meters.
- Placement examples: "Over the Rainbow"'s opening leap traces a rainbow; "Friends in Low Places" puts "low" on the lowest note; Lorde "Royals" puts the title first in every chorus line (unattainable status); Neon Trees "Everybody Talks" puts the hook last (rumor building).

## Drum patterns by genre

| Pattern | Kick | Snare/clap | Hats / percussion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Four-on-the-floor** (house/disco/techno) | Every quarter (128 BPM typical) | 2 & 4 | Offbeat open hats |
| **Rock / pop "money beat"** | 1 & 3 | 2 & 4 | Straight 8ths |
| **Boom-bap hip-hop** | 1, the and of 3, and 4 (16-step variant: kick on 1/9/11) | 2 & 4 (snare on 5/13 of 16) | Swung 16th hats, ghost notes, sampled break |
| **Trap** | 1 (and 4), deep 808 sub | 3 (half-time) | 16th/32nd rolls, triplets, pitch-shifted hats |
| **UK Drill** | Sparse, high velocity | Ghost notes on the last bar | Hat grid = "two spaces, two spaces, one space" repeated on the 1/16 grid (inherited from grime), plus rolls, triplets, and removals |
| **Funk** | Syncopated; "never quite landing on the one" (Stubblefield) or emphatically "on the one" (Brown) | 2 & 4 plus ghost notes | 16th hats, displaced backbeat |
| **Reggae: One Drop** | **Beat 1 empty**; kick plus cross-stick both on **beat 3** | With the kick on 3 | Eighth-note hats; offbeat accents |
| **Reggae: Rockers** | Kick on 1 & 3 | Snare 2 & 4 | Shuffled hats |
| **Reggae: Steppers** | Kick on all four downbeats (four-on-the-floor) | - | - |
| **Dubstep** | Varied | **Snare always on beat 3** (half-time) | - |
| **DnB** | Syncopated break | 2 & 4 (two-step) | Fast breakbeat, Amen-style |
| **Jazz** | Comping | Comping | Swung ride 8ths, hi-hat on 2 & 4 |
| **Afrobeat** | Syncopated | 2 & 4 with offbeat emphasis | Polyrhythmic percussion, shaker, congas/bongos |
| **Ska** | Walking | - | Offbeat upstroke "skank" |

One-drop grid (Wikipedia):

```
 HH |x-x-x-x-x-x-x-x-|
  S |--------x-------|
  B |--------o-------|
    1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +
```

Standard rock grid: kick on 1 & 3, snare on 2 & 4, straight 8ths on hi-hat.

Trap hat techniques (LANDR): **triplets** (felt in threes, not twos); **rolls** (double or triple the note value; 32nd rolls read clearly at trap tempo, 16th rolls are chunkier, 64ths blur); **swing** (makes duple sections fit the triplet context); **pitch automation** on hats to accentuate rolls. Trap is normally 4/4; 6/8 or 12/8 gives a "footwork-style" variant.

## Making a groove feel a mood

| Mood | Recipe | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Lazy / drunk / laid-back** | Delay snare and hats behind the beat (Dilla swing), leave hits unquantized, use quintuplet or septuplet swing, heavy swing at slow tempo, BPM 60-90 (lo-fi 70-90, boom-bap) | Myrtle Beach (Dilla: drum hits slightly late, usually snare and hats), Hein (quintuplet/septuplet = drunken), Orphiq |
| **Driving / urgent** | Four-on-the-floor kick, straight 16ths, faster BPM (120-150), trap hat rolls, no swing, high velocities | KickDrum.io, BandLab, Attack UK Drill, LANDR |
| **Heavy / half-time** | Perceived tempo halved: kick 1, snare 3, hats double-time; BPM stays high | Orphiq, Wikipedia Half-time, Musical U (dubstep) |
| **Calm / spacious** | BPM under 80, fewer subdivisions, more space between beats (reverb fills the gaps) | Orphiq, iZotope |
| **Hypnotic / trance-like** | Persistent ostinato ("riddim"), unvarying cycle | Wikipedia Groove (Herbie Mann), Splice (funk as trance state) |
| **Unstable / tense** | Syncopation, displaced backbeat, polyrhythm, odd meter | Hein ("Chameleon" displaced backbeat), Fretboard Anatomy |
| **Swinging / sensual** | Stretch onbeats, squeeze offbeats (ratio 54-66%) | Hein, Roger Linn |

Advanced: **metric modulation** changes the pulse by reusing a note value as a pivot. Formula: new tempo / old tempo = (pivot note-values in the new measure) / (pivot note-values in the old measure). Example: quarter = 84, and two half notes become three, so x/84 = 3/2, giving x = 126. Usually between mutually prime meters (4/4 into 3/8). Examples: Elliott Carter's Cello Sonata, Bjork "Desired Constellation" (dotted quarter = half).

## Where sources disagree (uncertainty flags preserved)

1. **Hip-hop BPM range:** Ableton says 60-100; iZotope 85-95; Musical U 85-115; Orphiq 70-100 (half-time 130-160). Resolution: 70-100 is the modern consensus for the perceived pulse; treat 85-95 as the boom-bap sweet spot.
2. **Rockers vs Steppers reggae labeling:** Wikipedia says rockers = kick every quarter and steppers = kick every eighth; Rhythm Notes says steppers = four-on-the-floor and rockers = kick 1 & 3 with snare 2 & 4. General practice and Rhythm Notes agree (steppers = four-on-the-floor); Wikipedia's "every eighth note" for steppers appears erroneous. Flag before teaching.
3. **"Swing = triplet":** not a constant. Ratio 2 is one point on a tempo-dependent curve (1:1 fast to ~3.5:1 slow), not the definition of swing.
4. **Groove = microtiming deviation?** Two aesthetic regimes, not a factual contradiction: "humanized" Black-American groove traditions versus the "aesthetics of exactitude" regime (where zero deviation scores highest for groove ratings).
5. **6/8 in trap:** LANDR treats 6/8 or 12/8 as a sub-genre variant (footwork-style); others treat trap hats as 4/4 with triplets. Compatible.
6. **Dance tempos:** Musical U's Salsa 180-300 vs Orphiq's Reggaeton 85-100 reflects different counting conventions (2/4 vs 4/4), not a true conflict.
7. **Vendor swing % vs measured swing ratio:** the 50-75% MPC scale is a machine parameter; the musical ratio is tempo-dependent. Both are valid and describe different things.

## How to apply this in Suno

- Put the BPM in the style prompt as a number, not a vibe: "92 BPM boom bap, swung 16th hats" beats "chill hip-hop beat". For half-time genres give both facts: "140 BPM trap, half-time, snare on 3".
- Name the drum pattern, because the pattern is the genre identity: "four-on-the-floor house", "one drop reggae", "funky drummer-style break", "UK drill hats".
- Describe swing explicitly when you want it: "swung 16ths", "shuffle feel", "lazy behind-the-beat drums". Say "straight 16ths, no swing" when you don't.
- Set the mood with the recipe table's levers: "half-time, heavy 808s, rolling hi-hats" for heavy; "sparse, slow, lots of space" for calm; "driving four-on-the-floor, offbeat open hats" for urgent.
- For odd meters, name the signature and the grouping: "7/8, counted 4+3" or "5/4 waltz-style, 3+2". Grouping matters as much as the signature.
- Keep lyric stresses on strong beats: put the title's stressed syllable at the start of a chorus line, and avoid long notes on throwaway words.
- Use "12/8 shuffle" or "6/8" in the style prompt for blues and doo-wop feels instead of hoping "swing" gets interpreted.
