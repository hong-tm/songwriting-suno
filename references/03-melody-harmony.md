# Melody and Harmony: Contour, Motifs, Hooks, Progressions, and Tension

How to build a melody that sings and a chord bed that moves: contour shapes, step-vs-leap rules, motif development, hook construction, functional harmony, progressions by use and mood, cadences, tension devices, borrowed chords, secondary dominants, modulation, modes, and how the melody sits over the chords.

## Quick rules

- Write more steps than leaps, at least **2:1**. A step is a 2nd; a leap is a 3rd or larger. Thirds barely register as leaps.
- After a leap bigger than a 4th, **change direction** and fill the gap with small steps. Big leap, then small step.
- Strong hooks are **simple melodic phrases repeated at least twice**. Repetition of short fragments beats novelty for memory.
- Chord tones on strong beats, non-chord tones as decoration. Build tension (V7, suspensions, 9ths) and release it to the tonic.
- **I-V-vi-IV** is the most popular four-chord progression in Hooktheory's 1,300-song corpus. In pop, **IV→I closes as often as V→I**; iii→I is rare.
- Keep the melodic core inside roughly **6-7 semitones** (two-thirds of pitches in chart pop), touching the extremes only briefly.
- Verse melody sits lower and narrower; chorus sits higher. Save the single highest note for the emotional peak.
- Modulate sparingly. The workhorse is a whole-step lift into the final chorus; "Man in the Mirror" lands its G→Ab lift on the word "change".

## Melodic contour

Contour is the up/down shape of the line. Five named shapes cover nearly everything:

| Shape | What it does | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Arch (hill) | Starts low, rises to one peak, descends back near the start; a sense of arrival and balance | Verses, balanced phrases |
| Ascending | Keeps rising | Optimism, rising action, building sections |
| Descending | Keeps falling | Release, calm, defeat, resolution |
| Wave | Several smaller rises and falls | The most common pop contour |
| Flat / recitation | Repeated single notes | Chant-like or rap-like hooks |

The classical reference rules (Bach-chorale style; the source says outright that exceptions abound): tendency tones resolve, 7→8 and 4→3, and 6/2 resolve down by step; avoid augmented intervals; a leap larger than a 4th (and any diminished leap) should change direction afterward; consecutive leaps should outline a triad. "The Star-Spangled Banner" opens with almost nothing but leaps, which is exactly why it is notoriously hard to sing.

Climax placement: a widely taught heuristic puts the highest note about two-thirds of the way through the verse, mirroring the golden ratio. It is documented for hymns and community singing, not measured in pop, and it is invertible (some melodies put the *lowest* note at the two-thirds point). Treat as a default, not a law.

## Range and tessitura

Empirical data, Hamilton & Pearce 2024 (top-5 Billboard songs per year, 1950-2023, hand-transcribed):

| Era | Avg melodic interval | Core range (⅔ of pitches) | Notes/sec | Notes/bar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1950-1974 | ~2.3 semitones | ~7 semitones | ~1.8 | 4.2 |
| 1975-1999 | ~2.1 semitones | ~6 semitones | ~2.0 | 5.0 |
| 2000-2022 | ~2.0 semitones | ~5.5 semitones | ~2.8 | 6.3 |

Revolutions were detected at 1975 and 2000 (strong) and 1996 (moderate), all of them complexity decreases plus rising note density. Melodic features move in one direction over time; they do not cycle back. Practical read: even 1950s pop keeps two-thirds of its notes inside a 6-7 semitone core. Write a tight core and touch the extremes briefly. (Caveat from the authors: top-5-only sample, small n, no value judgments.)

Community-singing guardrails (hymn pedagogy): stay within about 1.5 octaves; the comfortable zone is roughly B below middle C to D above it; never exceed A below middle C to top E for group singing.

Placement principle: an upward leap in a high register reads as more significant than the same leap low. John Legend's "All of Me" climaxes higher than any earlier statement of its 4th leap. Verse in a lower, narrower band; chorus higher; one reserved peak.

## Steps vs. leaps

- **2:1 rule:** most song melodies use at least twice as much stepwise motion as leaps, probably more.
- **Thirds blend seamlessly** and barely count as leaps in the listener's ear.
- **An upward leap of a 4th or more raises the emotional value of the lyric at that moment** ("All of Me" leaps up on "no kidding"). Downward leaps add interest instead: Jackson's "Man in the Mirror" chorus opens with one.
- **Gap-fill is the mechanism:** Narmour's Implication-Realization model and Huron's post-skip reversal both predict that a large interval is followed by small intervals in the opposite direction, filling the gap. This is the theory behind the "change direction" rule. Canonical examples: "Somewhere" (West Side Story, minor 7th leap then a step down), "Over the Rainbow" (octave leap then a semitone down).
- **Pop tension flag:** pop routinely breaks "change direction after a leap" for lift. It is a singability guideline, not a law.

## Motifs: repetition and variation

Seven canonical alteration devices, each with a named example:

| Device | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inversion | Flips the motif upside down (real = exact intervals; tonal = diatonic) | Bach, Invention No. 1 in C |
| Intervallic change | Same rhythm, some intervals different | "The Very Thought of You"; 10cc "I'm Not in Love" |
| Augmentation | Doubles every rhythmic value | Bach, Invention No. 1 |
| Diminution | Halves the rhythmic values | Holst, "Uranus" |
| Rhythmic change | Varies some rhythms, not all | "Push It"; Beethoven Pathétique |
| Ornamentation | Adds embellishing notes | (technique, any repertoire) |
| Extension / retrograde | Lengthens the motif, or plays it backwards | (technique, any repertoire) |

Related vocabulary: **repetition** = the same pitches again; **sequence** = the motif restated at a new pitch level (up or down a step); **imitation** = the motif restated in another voice. Memory rule (Ewer): if audiences don't remember the melody, incorporate repetition of short melodic fragments. Hooktheory's rule: almost every strong hook features a simple melodic phrase repeated at least twice.

## Call and response

A statement ("call") followed by a contrasting "answer", like a conversation. Uses: lead vs. backing vocals; contrasting instrumental figures; the same line restated in a different register or instrument; jazz "trading fours". It makes lines more memorable and underlies leitmotif-style character writing.

## Hook construction (worked examples)

A hook is the idea that reels the listener back in. It can be melodic, rhythmic, lyrical, or instrumental, and it can live in the intro, verse, or chorus.

- **Game of Thrones theme:** starts on the highest note, drops a 5th, climbs back in a repeating 4-note figure; toggles C minor against C major; restates the motif over new chords; adds call and response.
- **The Simpsons theme:** a stepwise staircase up and down plus a chromatic "hill", balanced by a four-note repeated-note figure.
- **Beethoven 5 / Queen "Another One Bites the Dust":** short-short-short-long rhythmic hook. Tap test: if you can tap it and someone recognizes it, it's a rhythmic hook.
- **Supremes "Stop! In the Name of Love":** the melody pauses on "STOP". Word meaning and melodic shape agree.
- **Pharrell "Happy":** long sustained harmony on "happy", over a simple repetitive lead.
- **Beatles "Don't Let Me Down":** a three-note descent on the word "down".
- **Deep Purple "Smoke on the Water":** instrumental riff hook.

## Melodic rhythm and prosody (the lyric joint)

- Stressed syllables are sung **higher, louder, and longer**, and they land **on the beat** (Pat Pattison).
- Prosody = all elements (melody, harmony, melodic rhythm, harmonic rhythm, lyric) working together to support the central message.
- Verse rhythms take shorter notes and more syncopation; chorus rhythms take longer notes with less syncopation.
- Rhyme maps to stability: perfect rhymes (fire/desire) and family rhymes (mud/truck) read as resolved; assonance rhymes (life/tide) read as unstable. Full lyric treatment lives in `04-lyric-craft.md`.

## Chord function in a major key

| Degree | Chord in C | Classical function | Tension |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | C | Tonic | lowest |
| ii | Dm | Pre-dominant | low-mid |
| iii | Em | Tonic prolongation | low |
| IV | F | Pre-dominant | low-mid |
| V | G | Dominant | high |
| vi | Am | Tonic prolongation | low |
| vii° | Bdim | Dominant | high |

Function flow (text version):

```
Tonic (I) → prolongation (vi, iii) → pre-dominant (IV, ii) → dominant (V, vii°) → Tonic (I)
Exceptions: IV→I (plagal) and V→vi (deceptive). From I you may jump to any function.
In minor: VII→III also works.
```

Terminology flag: other frameworks count three functions (tonic I/III/VI, subdominant II/IV, dominant V/VII) and stress that function is style-specific; a pop IV can behave very differently from its classical role. Same facts, different buckets. Quinn's triggers: tonic = degrees 1 & 3, subdominant = 4 & 6, dominant = 5 & 7.

## Progressions by use and mood

| Progression | Sound / use | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| I-V-vi-IV (C-G-Am-F) | The pop default; anthemic with a bittersweet dip | "Don't Stop Believin'", "Let It Be", "She Will Be Loved", "Edge of Glory" |
| vi-IV-I-V | Same four chords starting on the minor; moodier, modern | Ubiquitous pop rotation |
| I-IV-V (and I-IV-V-I) | Three-chord rock/blues backbone ("Rock 'n' roll is I, IV, V") | Blues, early rock |
| I-vi-IV-V | The '50s progression: tonic, prolongation, pre-dominant, dominant | Doo-wop era ballads |
| ii-V-I (Dm9-G9(13)-Cmaj9; minor: Dm7b5-G7alt-Cm6/9) | The jazz engine | "Autumn Leaves", "Tune-Up", "Pent Up House", "Lady Bird" |
| iii-vi-ii-V, all dominant 7ths (E7-A7-D7-G7) | Turnaround; enables tritone subs (E7-Eb7-D7-Db7) | Jazz standards |
| 12-bar blues: I I I I / IV IV I I / V IV I V (in C: C C C C / F F C C / G F C G) | Blues form; "shuffle" ending alters bar 12 to V-IV-I-I; "quick to four" puts IV in bar 2; dominant 7ths throughout | Blues, early rock |
| Minor blues: i7 i7 i7 i7 / iv7 iv7 i7 i7 / bVI7 V7 i7 i7 | Darker blues | Minor blues heads |
| I-V-vi-IV plus borrowed iv / bVI / bVII | Major key with minor-mode color, melancholy depth | "Spinning Wheel" walks I-bVII-bVI-V |

Pop data notes (Hooktheory, 1,300 songs): IV→I appears as often as V→I before the tonic, a real departure from classical practice; iii→I is rare (Em almost always moves to F or Am). Cited counter-example where iii→I works: Lady Antebellum "Need You Now".

## Cadences

| Cadence | Motion | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect authentic (PAC) | V→I, both root position, melody ends on 1 | Strongest close |
| Imperfect authentic (IAC) | V→I, weakened in some way | Weaker close |
| Half | Ends on V | Open, asks a question |
| Plagal | IV→I | "Amen", softer close |
| Deceptive | V→vi | Surprise, delay |

Pop note: the corpus data says IV→I functions as full closure in pop, not just as color.

## Tension and release

**Suspensions** are the cheapest reliable tension device: an accented non-chord tone held on a downbeat, prepared by the same note, resolved down by step. Types: 9-8, 7-6, 4-3, 2-3 (bass suspension), 6-5; inverted settings yield non-standard 5-4 and 3-2; reduce intervals larger than an octave. Named examples: Barber's Adagio for Strings (4-3), Handel's Alla Hornpipe (7-6 and 9-8), Bach chorale BWV 373 (2-3). The dissonance is already sounding when the harmony changes, so the release feels inevitable.

**Extension ladder:** triad → 7th → 9th → altered (b9/#9/#11), each rung adds tension. Adding the 7th to V sharpens the pull to I; the 7th of the scale is the key tendency tone, and release is that note reaching its goal. Jazz proxy for tension control: guide tones (the 3rd and 7th) define chord quality; a tritone substitution keeps the guide tones and swaps the root, recoloring tension without changing the resolution.

Tension ladder (low to high): I/vi/iii → IV/ii → V → V7, iiø7, vii°7 → 9ths and altered chords → suspensions on strong beats.

## Borrowed chords (modal interchange)

Borrow chords from a **parallel** mode on the same tonic (C minor chords inside C major). Most common borrows into major, from Aeolian: **iv, bVI, bVII**; also available: i, bIII, minor v, iiø7, vii°7. Direct substitutions (replace like for like, nothing else changes): IV→iv, IIm7→IIø7, V→Vm, VI→bVI, VIIø7→vii°7.

**Backdoor ii-V:** ivm7 → bVII9 → I (in C: Fm7 → Bb9 → C), a common, extra-final ending.

Examples: "Spinning Wheel" descends I-bVII-bVI-V; "Never Can Say Goodbye" borrows from D Dorian, D Lydian, and D Phrygian inside D major; "Days of Wine and Roses"; Brahms' Liebeslieder Walzer.

**Melody constraint (actionable):** borrow only where the melody note is common to both modes. Melody notes 1, 4, and 5 (C, F, G in C) admit the widest borrowed palette; the 7th degree (B) is the most restrictive.

## Secondary dominants

A major triad or dominant 7th that resolves to a scale degree other than the tonic, written **V/x** ("five of x"). V7/V is the most common. In C major: V/ii = A7, V/iii = B7, V/IV = C7 (the triad alone is just I; the 7th makes it chromatic), V/V = D7, V/vi = E7. Purpose: put emphasis on a diatonic chord. Tonicization shorter than a phrase = secondary dominant; longer = modulation. Extended dominants chain by fifths: V/V/V → V/V → V → I ("Sweet Georgia Brown" opens V/V/V-V/V-V-I).

**Two-step test for "is this chromatic chord a secondary dominant?":**

1. Stack the chord in thirds. It must be a major triad or a dominant 7th. If not, it is not one; check for a secondary diminished (vii°/x) or a passing / chromatic-approach chord.
2. Take the pitch a perfect 5th below the root. If that pitch is the root of a diatonic chord in the key, the chord is V/x.

Worked example: F# major (first inversion) in C. The 5th below F# is B, which is 5̂, a diatonic root, so it is V6/V. Counter-test: bVI7 in C is Ab7; the 5th below Ab is Db, which is not diatonic, so it fails step 2 and is modal interchange, not a secondary dominant. (A Lydian II such as D major in C is the same chord as V/V, which is why it can behave like one.)

Pop/jazz examples: II7 = V7/V in Dylan's "Don't Think Twice"; III7 = V7/vi in "The Shoop Shoop Song"; Beethoven's First Symphony opens on V7/IV.

## Modulation

Three broad types (Berklee): diatonic, chromatic, enharmonic. Mechanisms:

| Type | How it works |
|---|---|
| Common-chord (pivot) | A chord shared by both keys; the most common pivot is a predominant (ii or IV) in the new key. Requirements: quasi-tonic, modulating dominant, pivot chord. |
| Common-tone | One sustained or repeated pitch bridges the keys (good for 3rd-related moves) |
| Chromatic | One voice moves by half steps (C→C#→D); often distant keys |
| Enharmonic | Respell a chord (German 6th ≈ V7; diminished 7ths respell freely); reach any key in three or fewer chords |
| Phrase / direct / abrupt | New phrase, new key, no bridge. At song end this is the "truck driver's gear change" |
| Sequential | The motif restated at rising pitch levels until the new key is established |
| Parallel key | Same tonic, major to minor or back (mode change) |

Where the lift goes, with evidence:

- **Whole-step lift into the final chorus:** "I Will Always Love You" (Ab→Bb), "I Wanna Dance with Somebody" (Gb→Ab), Taylor Swift "betty" (C→D at the last chorus), "Getaway Car" (C→D in the bridge).
- **Repeated ascending lifts:** Beyoncé "Love on Top" (C→C#→D→Eb→E).
- **Modulate on the pivot word:** "Man in the Mirror" lands G→Ab on the word "change".
- **Pivot on bVII:** Beatles "Something" (A major bridge with bVII = G, then back to the home key).
- **Mode shift instead of key change:** "Hey Jude" ends F-Eb-Bb, i.e. F Mixolydian; Berklee reads it as throwing off safety without leaving F.
- **Contrast at every section boundary:** "Bohemian Rhapsody"; "Penny Lane" (verses B major to B minor, chorus A, final chorus B).
- On record: "modulation should be used in moderation".

## Modes and moods

| Mode | Difference vs. parent scale | Mood as described by sources |
|---|---|---|
| Ionian | = major scale | Bright, resolved |
| Lydian | #4 | Bright, ethereal |
| Mixolydian | b7 | Major with danger/darkness; rock, blues-tinged |
| Aeolian | = natural minor | Melancholic, expressive |
| Dorian | Natural 6 (raised 6) | Brighter than Aeolian; jazzy |
| Phrygian | b2 | Dark, Spanish |
| Locrian | b2 + b5 | Especially dark, unstable |

Five rules for writing modal melodies (Berklee):

1. Use all the notes of the scale. Omit degree 6 and Dorian vs. Aeolian becomes undecidable.
2. Use the characteristic note frequently (F# for Lydian, B for Mixolydian in G, and so on).
3. Emphasize the tonic: frequent, longer, on strong beats.
4. End with a melodic cadence 2→1 and/or 7→1.
5. Never leap or span the augmented 4th / diminished 5th; it implies the dominant 7 of the relative major and pulls you out of the mode.

## Circle of fifths

Clockwise = up a perfect 5th = add one sharp: C(0) G(1) D(2) A(3) E(4) B(5) F#(6) C#(7). Counterclockwise = add one flat: F(1) Bb(2) Eb(3) Ab(4) Db(5) Gb(6) Cb(7). Relative minors share the key signature: Am Em Bm F#m C#m G#m D#m A#m on the sharp side; Dm Gm Cm Fm Bbm Ebm Abm on the flat side. Order of sharps: F C G D A E B; flats reverse it: B E A D G C F. Fifteen named keys because of three enharmonic pairs.

Uses: neighboring keys on the circle modulate most smoothly; the V of any key sits one step clockwise; the circle progression vi-ii-V-I falls straight out of it.

## How melody sits over chords

1. **Chord tones on strong beats; non-chord tones decorate.** Non-chord tone catalogue: passing tone, neighbor, appoggiatura (accented, leaps in then steps), escape tone, double neighbor, anticipation, pedal point, suspension, retardation, incomplete neighbor.
2. **Melody constrains harmony.** When recoloring with borrowed chords, check which modes share the melody note (see the 1/4/5 rule above). Even a chord tone can clash with the melody: don't put a maj7 chord under a root melody note (a semitone apart); substitute a 6th.
3. **Melody-first vs. chords-first:** once a melody has good structure it guides chord choices; chords-first works but risks the melody taking a back seat (Ewer).
4. **Hooks interlock.** A hook works when the melodic phrase interlocks with the progression; melody and harmony are analyzed together.
5. **Avoid notes (jazz frame, genre-specific).** An avoid note is any scale note a half step above a chord tone. Per mode: Ionian avoid 4; Dorian avoid 6; Phrygian avoid b2 and b6; Lydian's 4 is technically one but sounds pleasing (why players use Lydian over maj7); Mixolydian avoid 4; Aeolian avoid b6; Locrian avoid b2. Per quality: Maj7 avoid 11/b9/b13; Min7 avoid the major 3rd, b9, b13; Dom7 "anything goes" (altered notes are used on purpose). Flag: this is a jazz improvisation construct; pop and rock use 4ths and b7s freely.

## Tools

- **Hooktheory TheoryTab:** 50,000+ songs (the trends page claims 75,000+) as chord + melody piano rolls; **Hookpad** keeps you in key and shows scale degrees; the 1,300-song study supplies the stats above.
- **Nashville Number System:** chords by number, 1 major, 2- 3- 6- minor, 4 5 major, 7° diminished. Heuristics: "the 5 always wants to go home to 1"; "when in doubt, 1-4-5 works"; "moving to the 6- adds emotion"; "4→1 is your friend for endings". Same idea as Roman numerals, optimized for fast transposition.

## Where sources disagree (uncertainty flags preserved)

1. **Three vs. four harmonic functions:** one textbook adds "tonic prolongation" for vi/iii, another uses tonic/subdominant/dominant only. Terminology, not substance.
2. **V→I vs. IV→I primacy:** classical texts make V→I the primary close; Hooktheory's pop data puts IV→I equal. Style-specific, both true in their domains.
3. **The leap rule vs. pop lifts:** "change direction after a leap" has two independent pedagogical sources plus the gap-fill rationale, yet pop breaks it for lift. Guideline, not law.
4. **"Hey Jude":** key change or F Mixolydian modal shift? Berklee presents both readings; its instructor concludes modal shift (tonal center stays F).
5. **The two-thirds climax heuristic:** documented for hymns and community singing; unmeasured in pop.
6. **Contour-shape prevalence** (descending, convex, ascending most prevalent in a folk-song corpus) rests on a truncated abstract; snippet-level confidence only.
7. **Avoid notes:** a jazz chord-scale device; pop/rock free use of 4ths and b7s contradicts it as a universal rule.
8. **bVI7 ambiguity:** often sounds like a secondary dominant; the two-step test above resolves it mechanically.

## How to apply this in Suno

- Suno writes the actual notes, so shape them with descriptors instead of notation: "arch-shaped verse melody, higher narrow-range chorus", "soaring upward leap into the chorus", "restrained low verse, belted high chorus".
- Name the harmonic language in the style prompt: "I-V-vi-IV pop anthem", "12-bar blues shuffle", "ii-V-I jazz standard", "Dorian folk", "Mixolydian rock" only when you want that color.
- Ask for cadence behavior when it matters: "pre-chorus ends unresolved", "suspenseful build into the chorus", and "final chorus key change" only if you actually want the truck-driver lift.
- Write the hook lyric as a short phrase and repeat it across choruses; Suno leans into what you repeat. Keep hook wording simple enough to sing at a glance.
- Control register by section with vocal descriptors: "breathy low-register verses", "full-voice high final chorus" (see `13-vocal-control.md`).
- For modal color, put the mode directly in the style string: "Lydian dream pop", "Phrygian desert rock", "Dorian minor verse with a major-lift chorus".
- Describe instrumental motifs in words: "descending three-note motif opening each bar", "call-and-response between vocal and guitar".
